THE STORY (keep to these facts)
- Great House Farm (Tŷ Mawr), Llandough, near Penarth, Vale of Glamorgan, Wales. The Williams family say they lived there for nine generations, from 1667.
- 1877: part of the farm was sold for limestone quarrying; the family says the deal promised the house back to them when quarrying ended. 1928: the last quarry machine left and rent on the house stopped — the family says the house parcel ("Parcel A") became theirs, and only the fields next door ("Parcel B") stayed rented, as the 1939 tenancy plan shows.
- 1955: the landlord (Western Ground Rents) took back the fields under a possession order against Mary's husband, but not the farmhouse: Mary Williams had just come home from hospital (the family says she had lost a leg; she used a wheelchair) and objected strongly.
- 1959: offered a tenancy of her own house — she refused, saying she owned it through her grandfather and documents proved it.
- 1962: a county court possession order — never enforced against the house. She stayed.
- 1965: offered a weekly tenancy at £2 — never signed, no rent paid.
- 1969: the land was sold on to BP Pension Trust, treating her house as included.
- 1974: another possession action — adjourned, her ownership never decided. She did not go to the press: the family says a court clerk tipped the newspapers off and the reporters came to her. She told them: "I will refuse to move… my solicitors tell me I have a valid claim to it."
- 31 October 1974: BP sent letters saying she "may remain" in the farmhouse "under licence". She never accepted. The family says she wrote back and that reply has never been produced.
- 1982–83: BP registered the land, house included, while she lived in it; the family says she was not told. 14 March 1983: Mary died, 19 days after the last registration application.
- 1987: the Court of Appeal (BP Properties Ltd v Buckler) ruled the 1974 letters made her occupation permissive "whether or not she accepted them" — so her years in the house never counted as ownership.
- 29 November 1988: the family was evicted; 6 December 1988: the farmhouse was demolished. Houses now stand on the site; an early-medieval cemetery of 814 burials was excavated there in 1994.
- Today her grandsons are recovering the files. Public bodies' replies have included "Not held", "Out of scope" and "Most likely destroyed".

THE ANGLE
An action/legal thriller: the family are ambushed again and again by attempts to turn an owner's occupation into mere permission, and each time Mary answers the same way — "This house is mine." The 1974 letter must play as a GOTCHA, not an agreement: the case is shelved, a letter drops through the door, she refuses it, and thirteen years later judges rule her refusal didn't matter. Mary is the hero: resolute, in a wheelchair, running a farm, never signing.
